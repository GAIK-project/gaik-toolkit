Let’s say that an actual magnification of two and a half in the Galilean loupes, like you have, is very suitable for a dentist. Two-times magnification is enough, or is generally better for a dental hygienist, because they often need a slightly wider field of view. Magnification works so that the more you magnify, the smaller both the field of view and the depth of field become. So you don’t want too much magnification if it compromises the other features. And with these Kepler loupes, the actual magnifications are perhaps between three and five, depending on the model. Those are the kinds of magnifications people work with. If it says seven times or eight times, you can take that figure with a pinch of salt.

Well, basically just as you said. In endo, we actually have quite a lot of users who do endo often, though not all the time, and they have this more powerful model for that, because it may not be quite as practical for general practice. It pretty much comes down to this: the finer the details you need to see, the greater the magnification. There’s no more complicated mathematics to it than that, but you do have to take those other features into account. That’s really it.

And then, of course, there are individual factors. If someone has very strong minus lenses, for example, they make the subject look quite a lot smaller, so it’s often worth choosing the next level of magnification up. Another thing that argues for more magnification is if someone is very tall and has a long working distance, or even works standing up. Then we’re generally talking about working distances of between fifty and sixty centimetres, and it may be worth choosing the next level up in magnification. But within both Galilean and Kepler loupes, we have many different options, and the final choice is always very individual.
