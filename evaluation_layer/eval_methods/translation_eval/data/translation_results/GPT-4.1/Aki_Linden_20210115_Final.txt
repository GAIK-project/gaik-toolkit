When you’ve already been doing this for quite a long time, there’s maybe that little element of uncertainty in the back of your mind, wondering whether this is really going to work or not. But the development is moving forward so quickly that I do see that in five years’ time, we’ll probably only be doing those marginal, more difficult cases with traditional impressions. Yeah, I’ve noticed the same myself, that my own difficult cases aren’t maybe the same as those of specialist dentists, but those are perhaps the ones I still want to do traditionally. But this digital impression-taking, especially now with all the software updates and everything, it’s just incredible how accurate they are, and it’s more comfortable for the patient, it’s faster, and in its own way even more reliable. If something goes wrong, you just take new scans, whereas with something like Impregum, you have to wait a while before you can take a new impression. That’s a good thing, and you can also add to it—if you missed something or whatever, you don’t have to redo everything, you can just add a part, and we can attach those, and so on. 

So maybe from the lab’s perspective, I’d say that intraoral or the digital world does require a bit of practice, because it’s quite easy for all those things related to making the product itself, and then all the libraries and so on, to go a bit overboard morphologically. The cusp inclines and everything else are quite large, and the structures can easily end up unnecessarily big and bulky. For example, we still do, at least until I learn enough, some of the planning traditionally, even though we already do a huge amount digitally. But for bigger occlusal rehabilitations or others, if the impressions have been taken digitally, we print the models and do the wax-up traditionally, because then we can see them in three dimensions much better than just on a two-dimensional screen. But from the dentist’s side, you can, when you get those models printed now—yeah, like I said, it’s not necessarily, you know, abroad there are already practices that don’t use traditional methods at all anymore, they’re completely digital, and they’re not just chairside practices, but they use them specifically as impression tools, which most labs receive. 

One more question about this before we move on: how do you see it, if you’re replacing a single tooth with an implant and you take a digital impression, is it necessary to scan the whole jaw, or is it enough to scan just the side where you’re working, say from the canine to the premolar area on the other side? Well, yeah, up to the canine on the other side is enough, and of course in marginal cases maybe just the segment is enough, but I would recommend going a bit past the canine, so you get some occlusal guidance there. At least if the bite is open in the front, that won’t solve anything.
