So let’s put it this way: an actual magnification of two and a half in the Galilei loupes, like the ones you have, is very suitable for dental use. A magnification of two is enough, or is generally better for a dental hygienist, because they often need a slightly wider field of view. Magnification works in such a way that the more you magnify, the smaller both the field of view and the depth of field become. So you don’t want to choose too much magnification, in case it compromises the other features.

Well, pretty much as you said: with endodontics, we actually have quite a lot of users who do endodontics often, but not all the time. They then have this model for that, with its higher magnification, because it may not be as practical when doing general dentistry. It largely comes down to this: the more fine detail you need to see, the higher the magnification. It’s not any more complicated than that mathematically, but of course you do need to take the other features into account.

And then, of course, there are individual factors. For example, if someone has very strong minus lenses, they make the object appear much smaller, so it’s often a good idea to choose one step higher in magnification. Another thing that argues in favour of more magnification is if someone is very tall and has a long working distance, or even works standing up. In that case, we’re generally talking about working distances of somewhere between fifty and sixty centimetres, so it may be a good idea to choose the next level of magnification. But even within these Galilei and Kepler loupes, we have many different options, and the final choice is always very individual.
