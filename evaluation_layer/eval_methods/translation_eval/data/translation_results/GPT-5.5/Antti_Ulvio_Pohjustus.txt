And then, well, as for those magnifications, it is a bit of a—just for fun, we always talk about marketing magnification and then the actual real magnification. Let’s put it this way: a real magnification of two and a half in those Galileans, like you have, is very suitable for a dentist’s use. Then a two-times magnification is enough, or is usually better for a dental hygienist, because they often need a somewhat wider field of view. And magnification works so that the more you magnify, the smaller both the field of view and the depth of field become. So it is not worth taking too much magnification, if it then impairs the other properties.

And then in these Kepler loupes, well, the actual magnifications there are perhaps talked about as being between three and five, different models within that range, so those are the kinds people work with. If it says seven times or eight times, then you can take that reading with a bit of a pinch of salt.

And then of course there are individual factors. For example, if someone has very strong minus lenses, they make the target quite a lot smaller, so then it is often advisable to choose perhaps one step higher magnification. And another thing that speaks in favour of magnification is that if someone is very large and has a long working distance, or even works standing up, then we are usually talking about working distances somewhere between fifty and sixty centimetres, and then it may be advisable to choose the one with one step more magnification. But even within these Galilean and Kepler loupes, we have many different options, and the final choice is always very individual.
