When you've been in the field for a long time, there's a small element of uncertainty in the back of your mind, wondering if this is truly effective or not. But the development is progressing so rapidly that I believe in five years, we'll only be handling marginally more difficult cases with traditional impressions. Yes, I've noticed the same thing myself, that my difficult cases might not be the same as those of specialist dentists, but those are the ones I still want to do traditionally. However, digital impressions, especially now with software updates, are incredibly precise, and it's more comfortable for the patient, faster, and in some ways even more reliable. If something goes wrong, you can take new scans, whereas with something like Impregum, you have to wait a bit before taking a new one. It's good in that sense, and you can add to it if you missed something, without having to redo everything. You can just add a part, and we can attach it there and so on. 

From the lab's perspective, I would say that the intraoral or digital world requires a bit of practice because everything related to product creation and all the libraries tend to be morphologically a bit excessive. There are quite large cusp inclines and other features, and structures can easily become unnecessarily large and bulky. For example, we still do some planning digitally, a lot actually, but for larger occlusal rehabilitations or others, if the impressions are taken digitally, we print the models and do the waxing traditionally because we can see them in three dimensions much better than just on a two-dimensional screen. But from the dentist's side, when you can now print those models, yes, as I said, not necessarily, abroad there are already practices that don't use traditional methods anymore, they're entirely digital, and they're not just chairside practices but use them specifically as impression tools that most laboratories send. 

One more question about this before we move on: how do you see it when replacing a single tooth with an implant? If a digital impression is taken, is it necessary to scan the entire jaw, or is it enough to scan from the side where you're starting, perhaps to the canine on the other side in the premolar area? Well, yes, to the canine on the other side is enough, and of course, in marginal cases, just the segment might suffice, but I would recommend going a bit beyond the canine so we can see the occlusal guidance. At least if the bite is open in the front, it doesn't solve anything.
